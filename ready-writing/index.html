PART 1 — Know the Contest

What happens

Your child gets two topics and picks the one they like best, then writes one paper

by hand. Each prompt says what to write and sometimes who it's for (like "a letter to your

parents").

  • Time: up to 2 hours is allowed, but 3rd–4th grade is usually shortened — plan for about 30–45 minutes.
  • Length: no required length. A clear, interesting paper beats a long, messy one.
  • Allowed: a dictionary or thesaurus.
  • Two must-dos: write the topic title at the top; never write your name or school.

Prompts at this level ask the student to describe something, tell a story, or give

an opinion and explain why.

How it's scored — the secret

Biggest tip: being interesting with real, specific examples matters MORE than perfect

spelling. Half the score is just good ideas. Never freeze on a hard word — keep the idea going.

PART 2 — The Writing Routine

Teach this one habit: PLAN → WRITE → CHECK

PLAN (about 5 minutes)

  • Read both topics. Pick the one you can tell a real story about.
  • Jot: my main idea in one sentence · 3 reasons/details · 1 real example.

WRITE (most of the time) — use this shape:

  • Title (make it fun — it's scored!)
  • Beginning: grab attention + say your main idea.
  • Middle: 2–3 paragraphs, one idea each, with a real example.
  • Ending: say your main idea again a new way; end with something to remember.

CHECK (last 3–5 minutes)

  • Read once for sense, once for capitals/periods/spelling.
  • Did I answer the exact topic? If it asked for a letter, did I start "Dear ___"?

Connecting words to keep handy: First, Next, Another reason, For example, Best of all, In the end.

PART 3 — Two Example Essays (see what "good" looks like)

These are written at a strong 4th-grade level. Read each one, then read the "Why this works"
notes. The point isn't to copy them — it's to see the shape and the specific details.

Example A — Topic: Unusual Pets

Title: My Pet Otter, Splash

If I could have any unusual pet, I would pick a river otter and name him Splash. Most kids have

cats or dogs, but an otter would be the most fun pet on my whole street.

First, Splash would need a big water tank, because otters love to swim and even sleep floating

on their backs. I would build him a pool in the backyard with a little slide, since otters slide

down muddy hills for fun. Every morning I would toss in fish for him to catch, because otters eat

about twenty fish a day.

Taking care of Splash would be a lot of work, but I wouldn't mind. Otters are smart, so I could

teach him tricks like stacking cups or juggling rocks with his paws. I would clean his pool every

week so the water stayed fresh and clear.

The best part would be our mornings together. I imagine Splash splashing me with his tail while I

laugh and get soaked before school. An otter isn't a normal pet, and that is exactly why I would

love one.

Why this works:

  • Interest (50%): a specific, original choice (an otter named Splash) and real otter facts (sleeps floating, eats ~20 fish, slides down hills). The title is fun.
  • Organization (35%): clear main idea up front, one idea per paragraph (home → care → the fun part), and a strong closing that circles back.
  • Correctness (15%): clean sentences, varied length, correct punctuation.

Example B — Topic: Watching TV (write a letter to your parents)

Title: A Letter About TV Time

Dear Mom and Dad,

I want to talk to you about how much TV I should watch each week. I think one hour on school

nights and two hours on weekends would be fair, and here is why.

First, having a TV limit helps me get my homework and reading done. If I know I only have one

hour, I finish my work faster so I don't miss my show. That way TV is a reward, not a problem.

Second, I would like to pick shows that teach me something, like nature shows or science ones.

Last week I watched a show about octopuses and learned they have three hearts. I shared that at

dinner, remember? Good shows can actually help me learn.

I know too much TV isn't good for me, so I promise to turn it off when my time is up. I hope you

think my plan is fair. Thank you for listening.

Love,

Your kid

Why this works:

  • It followed the exact format: it's a real letter ("Dear Mom and Dad," … "Love,").
  • Interest: a specific real example (the octopus show, three hearts) makes it personal and memorable.
  • Organization: a clear plan stated up front, then one reason per paragraph, then a polite closing.
  • Persuasion done right: argues one side (a fair limit) with reasons, which is exactly what the prompt wants.

PART 5 — Practice Prompts

The six official 3rd–4th grade prompts

  1. 1. Careers: The job you'd like to have when you grow up and why it's interesting.
  2. 2. Watching TV: A letter to your parents about how much TV you should watch and which shows.
  3. 3. And the best part is…: What you like best about school and why.
  4. 4. Friends are like stars: "Friends are like stars. You can't always see them, but you know they're there." Why are good friends important?
  5. 5. Unusual Pets: An unusual pet (no cats or dogs) you'd like, and how you'd care for it.
  6. 6. Homework: "Practice makes perfect." Write about your experiences with homework.

Extra practice prompts (same style, for more reps)

  1. 7. A perfect day: Describe your perfect Saturday from morning to night.
  2. 8. A new holiday: If you could invent a brand-new holiday, what would it celebrate and how?
  3. 9. Helping out: Write about a time you helped someone, and how it made you feel.
  4. 10. Best invention: What everyday invention is the most useful, and why?
  5. 11. A letter to the principal: Persuade your principal to add one new thing to your school (a club, a field trip, a lunch item) and explain why.
  6. 12. My favorite place: Describe a place that makes you happy so well that the reader can picture it.

PART 6 — Self-Check Scoring Sheet

After a practice essay, score it like a judge. Be kind and specific — point to real lines.

Interest (counts the most):

  • ☐ Has fun, original ideas (not boring or obvious)
  • ☐ Uses at least one real, specific example
  • ☐ Has an interesting title
  • ☐ Sounds like the writer's own voice

Organization:

  • ☐ Clear main idea near the start
  • ☐ One idea per paragraph, in a sensible order
  • ☐ Has a beginning, middle, and ending
  • ☐ Answered the exact prompt (and right format)

Correctness:

  • ☐ Capital letters and end punctuation
  • ☐ Spelling looks right (checked tricky words)
  • ☐ Complete sentences (no run-ons)

One thing to try next time: _________________________________

PART 7 — A Calm 3-Evening Plan (before Oct 27)

About 30–40 minutes each, encouraging, no pressure.

  • Night 1 — Learn it. Read Parts 1–3 together. Study the two example essays. Do one prompt

untimed, filling in a Planning Page out loud. Praise the interesting, specific parts.

  • Night 2 — First timed try. New prompt, ~30-minute timer, Planning Page first. Then use the

Self-Check sheet together: 2 wins + 1 fix.

  • Night 3 — Practice run. New prompt, full timer, no help. Read it aloud together at the

end — reading out loud catches missing words and run-ons best.

Your coaching cheat-sheet:

  • Praise ideas and real examples first (half the score).
  • Go easy on spelling — note one or two, move on (only 15%).
  • If stuck choosing a topic: "Which one can you tell me a real story about?"
  • The goal before a tryout is a calm, confident kid with a routine — not a flawless essay.
